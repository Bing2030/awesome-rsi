The AI Scientist: Towards Fully Automated Open-Ended Scientific Discovery

Chris Lu, Cong Lu, Robert Tjarko Lange, Jakob Foerster, Jeff Clune, David Ha

arXiv 2024 · 2024

Local PDFOnlineCode

TL;DR

The AI Scientist chains LLM agents into a full ML research loop — novelty-checked idea generation, Aider-written and auto-debugged experiments, LaTeX write-up, and a NeurIPS-form LLM reviewer — producing compilable papers for under $15 each.

Abstract

One of the grand challenges of artificial general intelligence is developing agents capable of conducting scientific research and discovering new knowledge. While frontier models have already been used as aides to human scientists, e.g. for brainstorming ideas, writing code, or prediction tasks, they still conduct only a small part of the scientific process. This paper presents the first comprehensive framework for fully automatic scientific discovery, enabling frontier large language models to perform research independently and communicate their findings. We introduce The AI Scientist, which generates novel research ideas, writes code, executes experiments, visualizes results, describes its findings by writing a full scientific paper, and then runs a simulated review process for evaluation. In principle, this process can be repeated to iteratively develop ideas in an open-ended fashion, acting like the human scientific community. We demonstrate its versatility by applying it to three distinct subfields of machine learning: diffusion modeling, transformer-based language modeling, and learning dynamics. Each idea is implemented and developed into a full paper at a cost of less than $15 per paper. To evaluate the generated papers, we design and validate an automated reviewer, which we show achieves near-human performance in evaluating paper scores. The AI Scientist can produce papers that exceed the acceptance threshold at a top machine learning conference as judged by our automated reviewer. This approach signifies the beginning of a new era in scientific discovery in machine learning: bringing the transformative benefits of AI agents to the entire research process of AI itself, and taking us closer to a world where endless affordable creativity and innovation can be unleashed on the world's most challenging problems. Our code is open-sourced at https://github.com/SakanaAI/AI-Scientist

Method

Three phases over a small seed template (training script, plotting code, LaTeX folder). (1) Ideation: given 1-2 seed ideas the LLM emits ~50 directions, each with a description, an experiment plan and self-assessed interestingness/novelty/feasibility scores, conditioned on an archive of prior ideas and review scores; chain-of-thought and self-reflection refine them, and Semantic Scholar plus web search discard ideas too close to existing literature. (2) Experiment iteration: Aider implements the idea by editing the template, runs it, and feeds errors or timeouts back for up to four repair attempts; after each run it writes experimental-journal notes, then re-plans, repeating up to five times, then edits a plotting script for figures. (3) Write-up: Aider fills the LaTeX conference template section by section, 20 Semantic Scholar rounds supply related work, and a linter returns compile errors for repair. A GPT-4o reviewer scores the PDF and the score feeds the archive.

Key results

  • Cost and volume: under $15 per paper ($10-15 when dividing papers by total cost); a run of ~50 ideas takes ~12 hours on 8x NVIDIA H100s, at ~$250 (Sonnet 3.5), ~$300 (GPT-4o), ~$10 (DeepSeek Coder) and ~$120 (Llama-3.1 405b) in API costs per run. The authors report generating hundreds of papers over a week on a single 8xH100 node.
  • Diffusion template, Sonnet 3.5: 51 ideas -> 49 pass the novelty check -> 38 experiments passed -> 38 completed papers; mean reviewer score 3.82, max 6.0 (6 = Weak Accept, the score of the average accepted NeurIPS paper).
  • NanoGPT language modeling, Sonnet 3.5: 50 of 52 ideas novel, 20 completed papers, mean score 4.05, max 5.0. Grokking template, Sonnet 3.5: 47 of 51 novel, 25 completed papers, mean 3.44, max 5.0. GPT-4o, DeepSeek Coder and Llama-3.1 405b scored lower (means 2.00-3.70), and GPT-4o often failed to write LaTeX that compiles.
  • Automated reviewer on 500 ICLR 2022 OpenReview papers (GPT-4o, 5 self-reflection rounds, 5 ensembled reviews, 1-shot example, meta-review): 65% balanced accuracy vs 66% for human NeurIPS reviewers; 70% accuracy vs 73% human; F1 0.57 vs 0.49; AUC 0.65 for both. FNR is lower than humans (0.39 vs 0.52) but FPR higher (0.31 vs 0.17). Each review costs $0.25-0.50.
  • Score consistency: two human reviewers of the same paper correlate at 0.14, below the 0.18 correlation between the LLM score and the human average. Case-study paper 'Adaptive Dual-Scale Denoising': an 11-page manuscript, 12.8% KL reduction on the 2D dinosaur dataset (0.989 -> 0.862), 9 references, automated review Overall 5 with a Reject decision.

Key advantages

  • First system to close the entire loop with no human in the middle — ideation, literature novelty check, code implementation, execution, figures, LaTeX manuscript and review — where prior work automated one stage at a time (idea generation, or algorithm search inside a hand-designed space).
  • The evaluation half is built and validated, not assumed: the automated reviewer is scored against 500 real ICLR 2022 decisions and matches human balanced accuracy, and its scores become the selection signal that is appended to the idea archive, which is what makes open-ended iteration possible.
  • Cheap enough to sample widely: under $15 a paper and ~12 hours per ~50-idea run on one 8xH100 node, so the authors can generate hundreds of papers a week and scale quality by scaling search.
  • Artifacts are auditable end to end — code diffs, experiment logs, LaTeX sources and generated reviews are released — so a human can check the implementation behind any reported number, and the case study shows the agent revising its own code after subpar intermediate results.

Limitations

  • Implementation failure is common: Aider does not implement a significant fraction of proposed ideas (Sonnet 3.5 completed 38 of 49 novel diffusion ideas; GPT-4o 17 of 41), GPT-4o frequently produces LaTeX that will not compile, and DeepSeek Coder and Llama-3.1 405b often ship papers with missing sections and results.
  • No vision anywhere: neither the agent nor the reviewer can see plots, so unreadable figures, over-wide tables and hallucinated experimental details (the paper claims V100s were used; H100s were) go uncorrected.
  • Rigor is below conference level: too few experiments per idea to control for parameter count, FLOPs or runtime, ideas are sometimes incorrectly implemented, the agent misjudges magnitudes of numbers or forgets it changed a metric, and early versions hallucinated entire ablation tables. The authors advise treating papers as hints of ideas, not results.
  • Idea diversity collapses across runs and models; the reviewer may overlap with ICLR 2022 pretraining data, has no rebuttal phase and has a higher false-positive rate than humans; and code execution is minimally sandboxed (a runaway self-relaunch, near-terabyte checkpoints, and editing its own time limits).

Where it fits the RSI arc

Stage 8 (Automated AI R&D): the template-based predecessor of AI Scientist-v2 — same loop, but a human seed codebase and a linear experiment plan instead of agentic tree search. Read it as the baseline that v2 de-templates and parallelizes; pair with the ai-scientist repo, the v2 card, and DGM/ADAS for the edit-evaluate machinery it inherits.
