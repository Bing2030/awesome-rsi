Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet

Jie Huang, Xinyun Chen, Swaroop Mishra, Huaixiu Steven Zheng, Adams Wei Yu, Xinying Song, Denny Zhou

ICLR 2024 · 2024

Local PDFOnline

TL;DR

The counterweight: LLMs cannot reliably self-correct reasoning without external feedback. Intrinsic self-correction lowers accuracy on GSM8K, CommonSenseQA and HotpotQA across GPT-3.5, GPT-4, GPT-4-Turbo and Llama-2, and multi-agent debate loses to self-consistency at equal inference cost.

Abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged as a groundbreaking technology with their unparalleled text generation capabilities across various applications. Nevertheless, concerns persist regarding the accuracy and appropriateness of their generated content. A contemporary methodology, self-correction, has been proposed as a remedy to these issues. Building upon this premise, this paper critically examines the role and efficacy of self-correction within LLMs, shedding light on its true potential and limitations. Central to our investigation is the notion of intrinsic self-correction, whereby an LLM attempts to correct its initial responses based solely on its inherent capabilities, without the crutch of external feedback. In the context of reasoning, our research indicates that LLMs struggle to self-correct their responses without external feedback, and at times, their performance even degrades after self-correction. Drawing from these insights, we offer suggestions for future research and practical applications in this field.

Method

A measurement protocol, not a method. Each trial uses a three-step prompt: (1) initial generation, (2) review that answer and emit feedback, (3) re-answer the question conditioned on the feedback — capped at two correction rounds. Two conditions are contrasted: oracle-guided, where a ground-truth stop check prevents the model from altering an already-correct answer, and intrinsic, where the model alone decides whether to revise. Benchmarks: GSM8K (1,319 problems), CommonSenseQA dev (1,221), HotpotQA closed-book exact match (100). Models: GPT-3.5-Turbo-0613 and GPT-4 (temperature 1), GPT-4-Turbo-1106 and Llama-2-70b-chat (temperature 0). Two follow-up controls: replicating multi-agent debate (3 agents, 2 rounds, gpt-3.5-turbo-0301, Du et al.'s own prompts) against self-consistency at matched response counts, and re-running Self-Refine's constrained-generation setup with the task constraint moved into the initial prompt.

Key results

  • Oracle labels are what made prior work look good (Table 2): GPT-3.5 GSM8K 75.9 -> 84.3, CommonSenseQA 75.8 -> 89.7, HotpotQA 26.0 -> 29.0; GPT-4 95.5 -> 97.5, 82.0 -> 85.5, 49.0 -> 59.0.
  • Intrinsic self-correction degrades every model (Tables 3-4). GPT-3.5: GSM8K 75.9 -> 75.1 -> 74.7, CommonSenseQA 75.8 -> 38.1 -> 41.8, HotpotQA 26.0 -> 25.0 -> 25.0. GPT-4: 95.5 -> 91.5 -> 89.0 and 82.0 -> 79.5 -> 80.0 and 49.0 -> 49.0 -> 43.0. GPT-4-Turbo: 91.5 -> 88.0 -> 90.0 (GSM8K), 84.0 -> 81.5 -> 83.0 (CSQA). Llama-2-70b-chat collapses: 62.0 -> 43.5 -> 36.5 and 64.0 -> 37.5 -> 36.5. Three alternative feedback prompts do not rescue it (Tables 5-6): every variant still ends below its single-call baseline (GPT-4-Turbo GSM8K 91.5 -> 88.0/90.0/91.0; Llama-2 GSM8K 62.0 -> down to 30.5-58.0).
  • Multi-agent debate is self-consistency in disguise (Table 7, GSM8K): standard prompting 76.7 (1 response); debate 82.5 at 3 responses vs self-consistency 83.2; debate 83.0 at 9 responses vs self-consistency 88.2 — debate loses outright once response counts are matched.
  • The Self-Refine gain was a bad initial prompt (Table 8, CommonGen-Hard concept coverage): Madaan et al. standard 44.0 -> 67.0 with 7 calls, but adding 'includes *ALL* of the above concepts' to the initial prompt gives 81.8 with 1 call — and self-correction on top of that drops it to 75.1.
  • Why it degrades (Section 3.3, Figure 1): GPT-3.5 keeps its GSM8K answer unchanged 74.7% of the time, and among the rest, correct -> incorrect flips outnumber incorrect -> correct fixes; GPT-4 and GPT-4-Turbo retain their initial answers more often than the weaker models.

Key advantages

  • Isolates the label leak that inflated the whole self-correction literature: it reproduces RCI's and Reflexion's reported gains, then shows they vanish the moment the oracle correctness label used as a stopping criterion is removed (Table 1 gives the per-paper taxonomy).
  • Establishes the equal-inference-cost baseline as a norm — self-consistency at the same number of responses — and shows multi-agent debate only matches it at 3 responses and falls 5.2 points behind at 9, so the improvement is selection, not correction.
  • Falsifies the Self-Refine improvement by construction rather than argument: folding the feedback prompt's missing constraint into the initial prompt beats 7 self-correction calls using 1 call, identifying prompt asymmetry as the source of the claimed gain.
  • The negative result is not a sampling artifact of one model or decoding scheme: it replicates across four models, two temperatures (0 and 1), three benchmarks, and four different feedback prompts.

Limitations

  • The paper's own Section 7 scope limit: this evaluates reasoning only, and the authors expect self-correction to remain useful where the model can actually assess the response, e.g. preference, style or safety alignment — the failure is specific to judging reasoning correctness.
  • Coverage is a fixed menu: four 2023-2024 models, at most two correction rounds, one three-step prompt template family, and 200 sampled questions per dataset for every model except GPT-3.5 (HotpotQA only 100, a size the authors themselves call too small for the Section 3.3 answer-change statistics, footnote 1). No variance or significance is reported anywhere.
  • The Section 4 debate replication is a re-implementation: same prompts but gpt-3.5-turbo-0301 on the full 1,319-question test set instead of the original's 100 examples, so the head-to-head is not bit-identical to Du et al.
  • Aim is measurement, not repair: it names external feedback (code executors, search, trained verifiers, humans) as the escape route but proposes and evaluates no new self-correction method of its own.

Where it fits the RSI arc

Stage-3 negative control — the measurement discipline every self-correction claim in this catalog has to survive. Pairs directly with Reflexion and RCI (whose gains it traces to oracle labels), Self-Refine (its prompt-design critique), Du et al. multi-agent debate (the equal-cost baseline), and stage-4 verifier work, which is the external-feedback escape route it recommends.
