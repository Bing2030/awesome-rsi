PAPER · stage 9 · Evolutionary & Open-Ended RSI

Paired Open-Ended Trailblazer (POET): Endlessly Generating Increasingly Complex and Diverse Learning Environments and Their Solutions

Rui Wang, Joel Lehman, Jeff Clune, Kenneth O. Stanley

GECCO 2019 · 2019

Local PDFOnlineCode

TL;DR

POET co-evolves a population of obstacle-course environments and their neural walker controllers in one run, transferring solutions between environments; it invents and solves challenges that neither from-scratch ES nor a direct-path curriculum can reach.

Abstract

While the history of machine learning so far largely encompasses a series of problems posed by researchers and algorithms that learn their solutions, an important question is whether the problems themselves can be generated by the algorithm at the same time as they are being solved. Such a process would in effect build its own diverse and expanding curricula, and the solutions to problems at various stages would become stepping stones towards solving even more challenging problems later in the process. The Paired Open-Ended Trailblazer (POET) algorithm introduced in this paper does just that: it pairs the generation of environmental challenges and the optimization of agents to solve those challenges. It simultaneously explores many different paths through the space of possible problems and solutions and, critically, allows these stepping-stone solutions to transfer between problems if better, catalyzing innovation. The term open-ended signifies the intriguing potential for algorithms like POET to continue to create novel and increasingly complex capabilities without bound. Our results show that POET produces a diverse range of sophisticated behaviors that solve a wide range of environmental challenges, many of which cannot be solved by direct optimization alone, or even through a direct-path curriculum-building control algorithm introduced to highlight the critical role of open-endedness in solving ambitious challenges. The ability to transfer solutions from one environment to another proves essential to unlocking the full potential of the system as a whole, demonstrating the unpredictable nature of fortuitous stepping stones. We hope that POET will inspire a new push towards open-ended discovery across many domains, where algorithms like POET can blaze a trail through their interesting possible manifestations and solutions.

Method

POET keeps a list of active environment-agent pairs, starting from one flat course and a random weight vector. Each iteration does three things. (1) Every paired agent takes one ES step (512-sample population, Adam, lr 0.01->0.001, sigma 0.1->0.01) maximizing forward reward in its own environment. (2) Every N_mutate iterations, environments whose agent scores >=200 become parents; their encodings are mutated (stump height, gap width, step height, step number, roughness genes), 512 children are generated, then filtered by a minimal criterion 50 <= E_child(theta) <= 300, ranked by novelty, and admitted until the active population cap (20) is reached; oldest environments are evicted as a queue. (3) Every N_transfer iterations, all agents are tried in all other environments: the best of the M agents plus M one-ES-step proposals replaces the target's champion if better (direct or proposal transfer).

Key results

  • Direct ES cannot solve the environments POET produces: for the five POET-created courses in Figures 2-3, the best of five ES runs of up to 16,000 steps each (twice the budget Ha [64] gives in the same domain) scores 17.9, 39.6, 13.6, 24.0 and 19.2, versus POET exceeding the 230 success threshold on each; ES agents converge on freezing before obstacles to avoid the -100 fall penalty. Single-sample t-tests give p < 0.01 on all five.
  • A direct-path curriculum control (flat ground, then one mutation-step increment at a time toward the target, advancing at the same score-200 threshold POET uses for reproduction, and given POET's full ES-step budget along the ancestral line) solves only the lowest difficulty tier; at very and extremely challenging tiers it falls short, and Mann-Whitney U tests show the distance-to-target grows significantly with difficulty level (p < 0.01).
  • Challenges are calibrated far above the OpenAI Gym Bipedal Walker Hardcore reference: difficulty gates are 1.2x, 2.0x and 4.5x the reference top-of-range values (stump height >= 2.4 vs 2.0, gap width >= 6.0 vs 3.0, roughness >= 4.5 vs 1.0). Three runs of 25,200 iterations with 20 active environments took about 10 days each on 256 CPU cores; mean iterations from creation to solve were 638 +/- 133 (challenging), 1,180 +/- 343 (very challenging) and 2,178 +/- 368 (extremely challenging).
  • Transfer is load-bearing, not cosmetic: in three runs there were 18,894 / 19,014 / 18,798 replacement attempts, of which 53.62% / 49.26% / 48.89% succeeded, and a no-transfer control solved zero extremely challenging environments while covering the environment space significantly worse (Mann-Whitney U, p < 2.2e-16).
  • Two-way transfer creates non-obvious stepping stones: a flat-ground parent whose agent walked in a crouch (a local optimum) mutated at iteration 400 into a child with small stumps; the child learned to stand and jump, and transferring that gait back at iteration 1,175 let the parent reach a score of 349 over 3,000 iterations versus 309 for the original gait, while the kneeling agent run for 3,000 iterations with transfer off never improved.

Key advantages

  • Demonstrates in a single run that an algorithm can invent, diversify and solve an increasingly hard set of problems that neither from-scratch optimization nor a directed curriculum reaches, with each claim backed by an explicit control (ES-alone, direct-path curriculum, no-transfer).
  • The acceptance gates are the mechanism, not the population alone: requiring the parent agent to have scored >=200 before reproducing, filtering children into the 50-300 minimal-criterion band, ranking by novelty and evicting oldest-first at capacity 20 lets POET keep children that are unsolved but improvable, which MCC would have deleted, so difficulty can rise faster than drift.
  • Backward (child-to-parent) transfer plus periodic all-pairs transfer attempts turns the population into an archive of reusable stepping stones; the paper shows a transfer can rescue an agent stuck at a local optimum that direct optimization provably never leaves.
  • The skeleton is domain- and optimizer-agnostic: ES is the inner loop here, but the paper argues any RL or search algorithm (PPO, TRPO, genetic algorithms) can be substituted, which is why the environment/solution co-evolution pattern recurs in unsupervised environment design.

Limitations

  • The problem space is bounded by the environment genome's maximum values (gap width, stump height), so difficulty can eventually 'max out'; the authors propose CPPN-style indirect or unbounded encodings as the fix, left to future work.
  • The agent's body is fixed, which caps the obstacles it can ever surmount (e.g. how wide a gap can be jumped); co-evolving morphology with the controller is future work.
  • The reward function is not evolved: one shared reward (forward progress minus hull angle and torque) applies to every environment, so POET generates new challenges but not new objectives, unlike the reward-generating work it cites.
  • Evaluation is a single 2-D bipedal-walker domain with only three runs, and the comparison is post-hoc: each POET environment is unique, so results rest on single-sample t-tests anchored to a single POET score rather than a fixed benchmark.
