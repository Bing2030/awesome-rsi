Learning to Learn by Gradient Descent by Gradient Descent

Marcin Andrychowicz, Misha Denil, Sergio Gomez, Matthew W. Hoffman, David Pfau, Tom Schaul, Brendan Shillingford, Nando de Freitas

NeurIPS 2016 · 2016

Local PDFOnline

TL;DR

The original proof that an optimization procedure can itself be learned. An LSTM is trained to act as an optimizer — ingesting gradients and emitting weight updates — by unrolling the optimizee's training.

Abstract

The move from hand-designed features to learned features in machine learning has been wildly successful. In spite of this, optimization algorithms are still designed by hand. In this paper we show how the design of an optimization algorithm can be cast as a learning problem, allowing the algorithm to learn to exploit structure in the problems of interest in an automatic way. Our learned algorithms, implemented by LSTMs, outperform generic, hand-designed competitors on the tasks for which they are trained, and also generalize well to new tasks with similar structure. We demonstrate this on a number of tasks, including simple convex problems, training neural networks, and styling images with neural art.

Method

Meta-learns an LSTM-based optimizer: it ingests the optimizee's gradients and outputs the weight update, trained by unrolling the optimizee's training and backpropagating through it. The learned update rule generalizes to unseen optimizees and tasks.

Key results

  • The learned optimizer generalizes to optimizees, networks, and task distributions it was not trained on.
  • Recovered hand-designed behavior (momentum-style updates) without being told to.

Limitations

  • Meta-trained on small, specific task distributions; scaling to large-scale training remained open (and largely still is).

Where it fits the RSI arc

The conceptual root of the whole list: if the *improver* is learnable, then the improver can be improved. Everything from TextGrad to DGM is a descendant of this idea.
