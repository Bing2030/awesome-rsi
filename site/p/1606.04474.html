Learning to Learn by Gradient Descent by Gradient Descent

Marcin Andrychowicz, Misha Denil, Sergio Gomez, Matthew W. Hoffman, David Pfau, Tom Schaul, Brendan Shillingford, Nando de Freitas

NeurIPS 2016 · 2016

Local PDFOnline

TL;DR

Learned optimizers: a two-layer coordinatewise LSTM consumes each parameter's gradient history and emits its update, trained by backpropagating through the unrolled optimization of sampled functions. It beats tuned SGD, RMSprop, ADAM and NAG on quadratics, MNIST and CIFAR-10, and transfers to unseen architectures.

Abstract

The move from hand-designed features to learned features in machine learning has been wildly successful. In spite of this, optimization algorithms are still designed by hand. In this paper we show how the design of an optimization algorithm can be cast as a learning problem, allowing the algorithm to learn to exploit structure in the problems of interest in an automatic way. Our learned algorithms, implemented by LSTMs, outperform generic, hand-designed competitors on the tasks for which they are trained, and also generalize well to new tasks with similar structure. We demonstrate this on a number of tasks, including simple convex problems, training neural networks, and styling images with neural art.

Method

The update rule is itself a parameterized function: theta_{t+1} = theta_t + g_t with (g_t, h_{t+1}) = m(nabla_t, h_t, phi), so a two-layer LSTM (20 hidden units each layer) maps the optimizee gradient at one parameter into that parameter's update. The LSTMs are replicated coordinatewise with shared weights but separate hidden states, making the optimizer tiny, invariant to parameter ordering, and applicable to tens of thousands of optimizee parameters -- a learned RMSprop/ADAM. Training minimizes L(phi) = E_f[sum_t w_t f(theta_t)] over sampled functions, backpropagating through the unrolled trajectory (Fig. 2); gradients along the dashed edges, i.e. d(nabla_t)/d(phi), are dropped so no second derivatives of f are needed. Setting w_t = 1 at every step rather than only at T makes trajectory prefixes informative. Gradients are preprocessed as (log|nabla|/p, sgn nabla) with p = 10, outputs rescaled; the optimizer is trained with ADAM, a random-search learning rate, and early stopping.

Key results

  • Setup: optimizers are two-layer LSTMs with 20 hidden units per layer, each trained with ADAM (learning rate from random search) and early stopping to avoid overfitting the optimizer, then compared against SGD, RMSprop, ADAM and NAG with per-problem tuned learning rates. Initial optimizee parameters are IID Gaussian.
  • Quadratics: on 10-dimensional f(theta) = ||W theta - y||_2^2 with Gaussian W and y, each function is optimized for 100 steps and the optimizer is unrolled for 20 steps, with no preprocessing or postprocessing; the learned optimizer substantially outperforms the tuned baselines (Fig. 4, left).
  • MNIST: the base net is an MLP with one hidden layer of 20 sigmoid units, gradients estimated from minibatches of 128 examples, 100 steps, 20-step unroll, LSTM outputs rescaled by 0.1. NAG, ADAM and RMSprop perform roughly equivalently while the LSTM optimizer beats them by a significant margin, and it still beats them when allowed to run 200 steps despite being trained for 100 (Fig. 4, center and right).
  • Generalization: an optimizer trained on the 20-unit sigmoid MLP keeps beating the baselines when the optimizee becomes 40 hidden units or two hidden layers, but changing the activation to ReLU makes the learned optimizer no longer able to generalize (Fig. 5); sweeping optimizee depth and width (1/2/5/20/35/50 layers, 5/20/35/50 units, Fig. 6) shows the best transfer exactly where test problems resemble training problems.
  • CIFAR-10: on a model of three max-pooling convolutional layers plus a 32-unit fully-connected layer with ReLU and batch normalization, one coordinatewise LSTM per parameter type (fully-connected vs convolutional) learns much more quickly than the baselines, with significant boosts on CIFAR-5 and especially CIFAR-2; an LSTM-sub trained only on the held-out labels transfers to the remaining labels. Neural art: trained on one style and 1800 ImageNet content images at 64x64 with 128 steps and a 32-step unroll, the optimizer reaches tasks of 49,152 parameters from 12,288-parameter training tasks, changing style, resolution and content simultaneously.

Key advantages

  • Establishes the optimization procedure itself as a trainable object: end-to-end BPTT through the unrolled optimizee trajectory (with d(nabla_t)/d(phi) = 0 to avoid second derivatives) produces a learned rule that outperforms hand-designed competitors on four task families even though every baseline gets its learning rate tuned per problem. This is the founding demonstration that 'the improver can itself be improved'.
  • The coordinatewise decomposition is the enabling trick rather than a detail: one small LSTM with shared weights and per-parameter hidden states scales to tens of thousands of optimizee parameters, is invariant to parameter ordering, and is exactly a learned analogue of RMSprop/ADAM -- so the learned object stays comparable to the rules it replaces.
  • Transfer is measured, not asserted: 12,288 parameters to 49,152 parameters at once, new styles and content images, MNIST to 40 units and two layers, and CIFAR subsets including an optimizer trained only on held-out labels. The paper's No-Free-Lunch framing makes the specialization claim principled rather than an admission.
  • The learned updates are inspectable and land on recognizable optimization behavior: Appendix B traces single coordinates and finds the LSTM takes larger steps than SGD and ADAM and uses momentum with a shorter time scale than ADAM, with a step-like update-versus-gradient response that is sometimes inverted (bigger gradient, bigger update).

Limitations

  • Generalization breaks when the dynamics shift, by the paper's own account: replacing sigmoid with ReLU activations makes the learning dynamics different enough that the learned optimizer can no longer generalize, and performance on a systematic depth/width sweep tracks how similar test problems are to the training distribution.
  • The architecture does not carry over unchanged: the coordinatewise LSTM was not sufficient for the convolutional model, which required splitting the optimizer into separate LSTMs for convolutional and fully-connected parameters, and the training objective had to be relaxed to w_t = 1 for all t because the true final-loss objective gives zero gradient on trajectory prefixes and makes BPTT inefficient.
  • Scale and horizons are small, and the optimizer itself overfits: training uses short unrolls (20 steps for quadratics and MNIST, 32 for neural art) requiring early stopping, and every experiment meta-trains on a narrow problem class (10-dimensional quadratics, one MLP, one CIFAR architecture, one style).
  • The more powerful variants are sketched rather than tested: global averaging cells and the NTM-BFGS / NTM-L-BFGS memory architectures (Appendix D) are argued to be able to simulate inverse-Hessian BFGS with one read head, three write heads and two GACs, but no experimental results are reported for them.
