POWERPLAY: Training an Increasingly General Problem Solver by Continually Searching for the Simplest Still Unsolvable Problem

Jürgen Schmidhuber

Frontiers in Psychology 2013 · 2013

Local PDFOnline

TL;DR

POWERPLAY grows a general problem solver from scratch with no external curriculum: it repeatedly searches for the simplest (task, solver-modification) pair such that the modified solver provably still solves every earlier task plus one the predecessor could not.

Abstract

Most of computer science focuses on automatically solving given computational problems. I focus on automatically inventing or discovering problems in a way inspired by the playful behavior of animals and humans, to train a more and more general problem solver from scratch in an unsupervised fashion. Consider the infinite set of all computable descriptions of tasks with possibly computable solutions. The novel algorithmic framework POWERPLAY (2011) continually searches the space of possible pairs of new tasks and modifications of the current problem solver, until it finds a more powerful problem solver that provably solves all previously learned tasks plus the new one, while the unmodified predecessor does not. Wow-effects are achieved by continually making previously learned skills more efficient such that they require less time and space. New skills may (partially) re-use previously learned skills. POWERPLAY's search orders candidate pairs of tasks and solver modifications by their conditional computational (time & space) complexity, given the stored experience so far. The new task and its corresponding task-solving skill are those first found and validated. The computational costs of validating new tasks need not grow with task repertoire size. POWERPLAY's ongoing search for novelty keeps breaking the generalization abilities of its present solver. This is related to Goedel's sequence of increasingly powerful formal theories based on adding formerly unprovable statements to the axioms without affecting previously provable theorems. The continually increasing repertoire of problem solving procedures can be exploited by a parallel search for solutions to additional externally posed tasks. POWERPLAY may be viewed as a greedy but practical implementation of basic principles of creativity. A first experimental analysis can be found in separate papers [53,54].

Method

Each round i, a search over program space P runs a candidate program p under a time slice to do three jobs: TASK INVENTION computes a task T (e.g. T = (I, O, t, n), solved iff L(s) < n and s reads I, emits O, halts within t steps); SOLVER MODIFICATION computes q by editing s_{i-1}; CORRECTNESS DEMONSTRATION must show s_{i-1} cannot solve T while q solves T and all T_1..T_{i-1}. The first validated triple is kept - p_i, s_i, T_i, Trace_i go into read-only storage that later programs may read and re-use - then the loop repeats. Ordering is Levin-style universal search: prior P(p) = 2^-L(p), time limit geometrically doubled (tlim := 2*tlim), so short and quickly verifiable pairs are tried first. Self-delimiting prefix-code solvers (OOPS) make extending s_{i-1} provably safe, and per-component task lists L_k restrict re-testing to tasks that use the changed components.

Key results

  • Optimality of the OOPS instantiation (Alg. 4.1): if some unknown optimal program p solves a problem instance of size k and demonstrates correctness in f(k) steps, the search needs at most O(f(k)/P(p)) = O(f(k)) steps - the 1/P(p) constant factor may be large but does not depend on k. Programs are ordered by P(p) = 2^-L(p), and the time limit doubles each pass (tlim := 2*tlim), so no horizon has to be set.
  • The acceptance gate is the whole result: (1) the new task is unsolvable by s_{i-1}; (2) it is solvable by q; (3) q still solves T_1..T_{i-1}. Because s_{i-1}'s used components are frozen (prefix-code extension), this gives a by-design no-forgetting guarantee, and - per the abstract - the computational costs of validating new tasks need not grow with task repertoire size.
  • Tasks carry explicit resource bounds: T_i = (I_i, O_i, t_i, n_i) is solved only if L(s_i) < n_i and at most t_i discrete steps are needed to read I_i, compute O_i and halt. A task may instead demand a wow-effect - solving a previously solved T_k faster, t_i < t_k − ε - which makes compression/speedup of earlier solutions a first-class self-invented task rather than a separate objective.
  • Variant II (Alg. 7.1) replaces the binary gate with a cost gate: accept only when c*_i − c_i > ε, where Cost(s, T_SET) = L(s) + α·Σ over T in T_SET of [t'_s(T) − r(T)], α weighing space against time and the reward r_new for novel self-generated tasks exceeding t_max. Individual solutions may then be forgotten as long as average performance improves. The paper notes the underlying universal-search trade of one extra bit of space complexity for a runtime speedup factor of 2.
  • There are no experiments in this paper: Section 8 points to companion papers [53] (IEEE ICDL-EpiRob 2012) and [52] (arXiv:1210.8385), where SLIM RNN problem solvers continually add self-invented problems, self-modularize by re-using code, and exhibit developmental stages. Everything reported here is the framework and its formal guarantees.

Key advantages

  • No-forgetting is structural, not empirical: freezing used components of s_{i-1} and extending only via self-delimiting prefix codes means earlier tasks stay solvable by induction, so POWERPLAY cannot suffer the online-learning problems the paper attributes to its 1997-2002 co-evolving system. Validation stays cheap because component lists L_k (Section 3.3.2) confine re-testing to tasks whose solutions actually touch the changed parts - the paper argues divide-and-conquer/self-modularization emerges as a by-product of the drive to validate quickly.
  • Enlarging the search space from solvers to (task, solver) pairs can shorten the wait between solver improvements, not lengthen it: inventing a simple task with an easy-to-find solution is often cheaper than improving on a fixed hard task, and greedy complexity ordering automatically trades off inventing a new unsolvable problem against compressing or speeding up previous solutions.
  • It is deliberately practical rather than asymptotically optimal: unlike the Godel machine and Hsearch, whose constant slowdowns make them infeasible on real-world problem sizes, POWERPLAY is meant to exploit a specific architecture (PC, RNN, SLIM NN) including the constants that asymptotic notation discards; the cost variant also accommodates externally supplied tasks and a probabilistic variant handles non-stationary, partially observable environments.
  • The framework is a clean plug-in point: TASK INVENTION, SOLVER MODIFICATION and CORRECTNESS DEMONSTRATION are separable, with concrete instantiations given for optimal program search over a FORTH-like language (existing OOPS source code) and for black-box/evolutionary search over RNN weight matrices, plus an explicitly self-referential mode where the current solver helps solve the search for its own successor.

Limitations

  • Greed is acknowledged as the core weakness (Section 9.3, drawback 1): always taking the easiest-to-find and easiest-to-validate task may sacrifice large long-term gains for small short-term ones, and a stream of easily solvable tasks may at least temporarily prevent it from learning hard ones. The paper's mitigation is only an expectation - on RNNs with finite capacity it should soon run out of easy unsolved tasks.
  • It keeps only a selective list of previously solved tasks and discards the history of still-unsolved ones (drawback 2), and Variant I's accept/reject criterion is binary rather than information-theoretic (drawback 3); the cost-based Variant II is offered as the fix, not as a validated one.
  • The strong guarantee is intractable in general: the most general CORRECTNESS DEMONSTRATION is proof search over an axiomatic system describing every instruction's effect on the solver state (Section 3.3.1), i.e. this framework's version of the Godel machine's proof-search bottleneck; the cheaper conditions are special cases (frozen components, prefix codes).
  • Nothing here is experimentally validated by this paper - Section 5 and Section 6 both end by saying experiments are needed to analyze when the system runs out of trivial tasks and when self-generated tasks actually accelerate externally posed ones; Section 10 also warns that an unrestricted variant with access to physical devices or extra computational resources is hard to predict, and that unchecked curiosity may be harmful to the system itself.
