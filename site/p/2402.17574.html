Agent-Pro: Learning to Evolve via Policy-Level Reflection and Optimization

Wenqi Zhang, Ke Tang, Hai Wu, Mengna Wang, Yongliang Shen, Guiyang Hou, Zeqi Tan, Peng Li, Yueting Zhuang, Weiming Lu

ACL 2024 · 2024

Local PDFOnline

TL;DR

Agent-Pro evolves an LLM agent's behavioral policy — its prompt-level beliefs and decision rules — through in-game reflection and payoff-based selection, letting GPT-4 beat the trained DMC poker bot in Limit Texas Hold'em with zero parameter updates.

Abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) exhibit robust problem-solving capabilities for diverse tasks. However, most LLM-based agents are designed as specific task solvers with sophisticated prompt engineering, rather than agents capable of learning and evolving through interactions. These task solvers necessitate manually crafted prompts to inform task rules and regulate LLM behaviors, inherently incapacitating to address complex dynamic scenarios e.g., large interactive games. In light of this, we propose Agent-Pro: an LLM-based Agent with Policy-level Reflection and Optimization that can learn a wealth of expertise from interactive experiences and progressively elevate its behavioral policy. Specifically, it involves a dynamic belief generation and reflection process for policy evolution. Rather than action-level reflection, Agent-Pro iteratively reflects on past trajectories and beliefs, fine-tuning its irrational beliefs for a better policy. Moreover, a depth-first search is employed for policy optimization, ensuring continual enhancement in policy payoffs. Agent-Pro is evaluated across two games: Blackjack and Texas Hold'em, outperforming vanilla LLM and specialized models. Our results show Agent-Pro can learn and evolve in complex and dynamic scenes, which also benefits numerous LLM-based applications.

Method

Policy here means the prompt itself: task description plus behavioral strategy. 'Training' is gradient-free prompt optimization through play. Each decision, the agent first writes natural-language beliefs — self-belief (hand, plan, risk) and world-belief (opponents, rules) — then acts conditioned on them. After lost games, policy-level reflection audits the whole belief sequence against the delayed final result (correctness, consistency, rationality), not single actions, and distills failures into two instruction types: Behavioral Guidelines (goals, strategies, demonstration trajectories) and World Modeling (opponent models, overlooked rules). New instructions are verified by replaying the same game (kept only if the score improves, ≤3 retries), then evaluated on novel games with all hands and seats swapped across (K+1)² permutations; the metric Δ is the average margin over the strongest opponent. A depth-first search over B=8 candidate policies per iteration accepts any Δ gain and backtracks otherwise — in-context policy iteration.

Key results

  • Blackjack, 900 games vs dealer (Table 1): win rate 36.2% on Qwen-72B (+3.9 over best baseline Reflexion 32.3), 23.1% on Llama2-70B (+11.0 over Reflexion's 12.1), 38.2% on GPT-3.5 (+1.5); on GPT-4 it reaches 40.4%, 0.5 below plain ReAct (40.9). Paper's overall claim: ~+4% average advantage; GPT-3.5 Agent-Pro tops the human reference (37.1%, Table C3).
  • Limit Hold'em, 1600 test games (Table 2), GPT-4 base: Agent-Pro averages 4.3 chips per game vs Vanilla LLM 1.4 (+2.9), beating the trained DMC bot (1.1, +3.2), ReAct (2.2) and Reflexion (1.2); human reference 5.7. GPT-3.5 base: 1.2 vs −1.1 (+2.3); Llama2-70B: −1.4 vs −2.2 (+0.8) — average gain ~+2 chips.
  • Ablations on GPT-4 (Table B2): belief-aware decisions alone (no learning) score 2.7 chips (+1.3 over vanilla); reflection without the belief module drops to 2.5 and yields vague, verbose instructions — beliefs help both deciding and reflecting.
  • Few-shot demonstration does not substitute for evolution: adding 3 winning example hands to the vanilla prompt scores 0.9 chips (−0.5), 3 losing hands 2.0 (+0.6), both far below the evolved policy's 4.3.
  • Learned styles diverge by base model when dealt the same bad hand: GPT-4-based Agent-Pro bluffs and probes, GPT-3.5 plays cautiously and folds late, Llama2-70B collapses into folding at the start (§5.2, Fig 4-5).

Key advantages

  • Shifts reflection from the action level to the policy level: it checks belief trajectories against delayed, game-level outcomes and compiles them into reusable instructions, which is exactly where action-level correction (Reflexion) stalls in long-horizon zero-sum play — e.g. Llama2-70B Blackjack 23.1% vs 12.1%.
  • Verification-plus-swap evaluation is a genuine in-context analog of policy iteration: a new prompt is accepted only when Δ improves on novel games with hands and seats permuted (K+1)² times, making gains luck-free and monotonic without any gradient step.
  • Shows strategic behaviors — bluffing, disguising strength, cutting losses — emerging purely from reflection on ~167 curated losing hands, and demonstrates that winning demonstrations are useless as prompts while failures drive reflection.
  • Ablates its own load-bearing parts: the dynamic belief module improves decisions even without learning (+1.3 chips) and is also necessary for high-quality reflection (4.3 → 2.5 without it).

Limitations

  • Paper's own: heavy dependency on the base model — the GPT-4-based agent nears human level and beats DMC, but GPT-3.5 and Llama2-70B versions remain below DMC despite notable gains.
  • Paper's own: a significant gap likely remains to state-of-the-art game algorithms such as CFR+; no head-to-head against such solvers is run.
  • Evidence covers only two RLCard card games; Blackjack learning is simplified (50 failed games, no verification or DFS), and Hold'em evaluation rests on 100 test hands × 16 permutations.
  • On the simpler game the ceiling shows: with GPT-4, Agent-Pro (40.4%) does not beat plain ReAct (40.9%) at Blackjack, and the paper notes different strategies perform 'quite similarly' there.

Where it fits the RSI arc

Moves self-evolution from prompt tweaks to the decision layer: the evolving artifact is a behavioral policy — beliefs plus decision rules — verified and selected by in-game payoff, i.e. gradient-free policy iteration for LLM agents (stage 5, harness-level). A direct precursor to policy-level RL-agent evolution, and an early case of LLM-judged evolution beating trained RL opponents.
