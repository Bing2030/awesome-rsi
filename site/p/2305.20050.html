Let's Verify Step by Step

Hunter Lightman, Vineet Kosaraju, Yura Burda, Harri Edwards, Bowen Baker, Teddy Lee, Jan Leike, John Schulman, Ilya Sutskever, Karl Cobbe

ICLR 2024 · 2024

Local PDFOnline

TL;DR

Process supervision — human labels on every reasoning step — trains reward models that beat outcome supervision on MATH: the PRM solves 78.2% best-of-1860 vs 72.4% for the ORM, and active learning makes step-level labelling 2.6x more data-efficient.

Abstract

In recent years, large language models have greatly improved in their ability to perform complex multi-step reasoning. However, even state-of-the-art models still regularly produce logical mistakes. To train more reliable models, we can turn either to outcome supervision, which provides feedback for a final result, or process supervision, which provides feedback for each intermediate reasoning step. Given the importance of training reliable models, and given the high cost of human feedback, it is important to carefully compare the both methods. Recent work has already begun this comparison, but many questions still remain. We conduct our own investigation, finding that process supervision significantly outperforms outcome supervision for training models to solve problems from the challenging MATH dataset. Our process-supervised model solves 78% of problems from a representative subset of the MATH test set. Additionally, we show that active learning significantly improves the efficacy of process supervision. To support related research, we also release PRM800K, the complete dataset of 800,000 step-level human feedback labels used to train our best reward model.

Method

Humans label generator-produced MATH solutions step by step as positive, negative, or neutral (neutral = reasonable but non-progressing), and labelling stops at the first mistake so the cost matches outcome labelling. A PRM is the MathMix-finetuned base model trained to emit that label token after each step; at test time a single forward pass gives per-step probabilities, and the solution score is their product. Data collection is active: a small PRM_selector ranks 1000 samples per problem and 80% of surfaced solutions are its highest-scoring wrong-answer ones (20% other convincing samples), with the PRM retrained between generations. The ORM baseline instead predicts solution correctness at the final token. The generator itself is never RL-trained; only the reward models are compared, judged by best-of-N search over uniformly sampled solutions.

Key results

  • MATH, 500 held-out problems, best-of-1860 over generator samples: PRM 78.2% vs ORM 72.4% vs majority voting 69.6% (Fig. 3). The PRM leads at every N and the gap widens as N grows, while majority voting is a strong but beaten baseline.
  • Scoring-strategy sweep (Table 4, best-of-1860): product of step scores with neutrals counted positive 78.2%, product with neutrals negative 77.4%, minimum reduction 77.6% / 77.8% — all within 0.8 points, so the win is not an artifact of the reduction rule.
  • Controlled small-scale comparison on identical data (1-200 samples per problem, Fig. 4): process supervision beat both outcome-supervision forms at every data scale, and PRM_large-scored outcome supervision beat final-answer-checked outcome supervision — the gap is exactly the right-answer/wrong-reasoning false positives.
  • Active learning (80% convincing wrong-answer + 20% other convincing samples) is ~2.6x more data-efficient than uniform labelling, estimated from the slopes of the best-of-500 fit; gains flatten at 200 samples/problem because that is already a large fraction of the 1000-sample selection pool.
  • Out-of-distribution held-out AP Calculus/Chemistry/Physics + AMC10/12 set, best-of-100 (Table 1): aggregate PRM 72.9% vs ORM 63.8% vs majority 61.3%; AMC10/12 53.2% vs 49.1% vs 32.8%. PRM800K is released: 800K step-level labels over 75K solutions and 12K problems (phase 1 is ~5% of it, ~40K labels).

Key advantages

  • A negative alignment tax: unlike most safety-motivated methods, process supervision improves reliability and capability at the same time, so adopting it does not cost accuracy — the authors argue this should raise its adoption and its alignment side-effects.
  • Precise credit assignment is the named mechanism: an outcome label says only that a solution is wrong, while step labels say which prefix was right and where the first error is, and the difficulty-quintile breakdown shows the PRM stays robust (and gap persists) across all five quintiles while the ORM degrades on the easiest one as N grows.
  • Active learning plus the released PRM800K removes the main barrier to entry — human step-level labelling — and PRM_large is shown to work as a cheap labelling oracle, making large data-collection ablations feasible at small scale.
  • The result holds under distribution shift: on fresh, post-pretraining AP/AMC exams the PRM beats both the ORM and majority voting, so the advantage is not only an in-distribution artifact.

Limitations

  • The large-scale head-to-head is confounded by construction: the ORM trains on 100 uniformly sampled solutions per problem while the PRM trains on an active-learning-biased, order-of-magnitude smaller PRM800K, so Section 3 is not apples-to-apples — the controlled Section 4 comparison is the real evidence.
  • Scope is mathematical reasoning only, and the generator is deliberately never RL-trained; the paper makes no claim about process reward models inside an RLHF or self-improvement loop, which it calls a natural next step but explicitly out of scope.
  • MATH test-set contamination cannot be ruled out: the test problems appear online and string-matching was not a guarantee, so some inflation is possible (though the authors argue it would hit all methods similarly, and the uncontaminated OOD results support that).
  • Active learning underperforms its own trend at 200 samples/problem, and iteratively retraining PRM_selector between generations was unstable with no performance gain — the authors call iterative retraining compelling but unsupported.
