Towards Execution-Grounded Automated AI Research

Chenglei Si, Zitong Yang, Yejin Choi, Emmanuel Candès, Diyi Yang, Tatsunori Hashimoto

arXiv 2026 · 2026

Local PDFOnline

TL;DR

Three-part automated executor (Implementer/Scheduler/Worker) turns natural-language ideas into real GPU experiments on nanoGPT pre-training and GRPO post-training, then feeds results back as reward: execution-guided evolutionary search beats baselines within ten epochs; GRPO RL lifts average reward but collapses diversity and never raises the maximum.

Abstract

Automated AI research holds great potential to accelerate scientific discovery. However, current LLMs often generate plausible-looking but ineffective ideas. Execution grounding may help, but it is unclear whether automated execution is feasible and whether LLMs can learn from the execution feedback. To investigate these, we first build an automated executor to implement ideas and launch large-scale parallel GPU experiments to verify their effectiveness. We then convert two realistic research problems - LLM pre-training and post-training - into execution environments and demonstrate that our automated executor can implement a large fraction of the ideas sampled from frontier LLMs. We analyze two methods to learn from the execution feedback: evolutionary search and reinforcement learning. Execution-guided evolutionary search is sample-efficient: it finds a method that significantly outperforms the GRPO baseline (69.4% vs 48.0%) on post-training, and finds a pre-training recipe that outperforms the nanoGPT baseline (19.7 minutes vs 35.9 minutes) on pre-training, all within just ten search epochs. Frontier LLMs often generate meaningful algorithmic ideas during search, but they tend to saturate early and only occasionally exhibit scaling trends. Reinforcement learning from execution reward, on the other hand, suffers from mode collapse. It successfully improves the average reward of the ideator model but not the upper-bound, due to models converging on simple ideas. We thoroughly analyze the executed ideas and training dynamics to facilitate future efforts towards execution-grounded automated AI research.

Method

Two environments, each a baseline codebase with fixed data, benchmark and metric: nanoGPT speedrun (train 124M GPT-2 on FineWeb toward validation loss 3.28 on 8 H100s, proxied during search as 1/loss under a fixed 25-minute budget) and GRPO (finetune Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B on MATH; metric is max MATH validation accuracy; validation code sits in a file the executor cannot touch). Three executor blocks: Implementer prompts a code-LLM with idea plus baseline to sample 10 code diffs in parallel, self-revising unappliable patches up to twice and returning the first that applies; Scheduler pulls codebases on a fixed clock and sizes each job to the environment's GPU requirement; Worker runs the job and uploads logs, metadata and metrics to wandb. The ideator reads back each idea's measured result. Search mixes exploitation (variants of ideas beating baseline beta) with exploration, annealed from 50/50; RL finetunes Qwen3-30B-A3B with GRPO on the same executed reward.

Key results

  • Execution is feasible at scale: self-execution completion rate is 84% for Claude-4.5-Sonnet on GRPO and 90% on nanoGPT, falling to 42% and 78% when GPT-5 executes Sonnet's ideas; with GPT-5 fixed as executor every ideator lands between 42% and 78%, and best-of-N (N=50) still beats baseline - Claude-4.5-Sonnet reaches max accuracy 60.4% vs the 48.0% GRPO baseline, Claude-4.5-Opus min loss 3.237 vs the 3.255 nanoGPT baseline, Qwen3-235B 50.2% and 3.238.
  • Execution-guided search wins: within ten epochs Claude-4.5-Sonnet hits 69.4% on GRPO at epoch 2 (baseline 48.0%), beating the best Stanford CS336 student solution at 68.8%; Claude-4.5-Opus reaches min validation loss 3.1407 at epoch 9, and rerun on 8 H100s that recipe reaches the 3.28 target in 19.7 minutes vs 35.9 minutes for the baseline codebase (human speedrun record: 2.1 minutes).
  • The feedback is load-bearing, not resampling: under an identical sampling budget (N=80 per epoch) GPT-5's execution-guided search beats best-of-N at N in {80, 160, 240} from epoch 1 onward, starting from the same epoch-0 performance.
  • RL from execution reward raises the mean but not the ceiling: average accuracy on GRPO goes 0.253 to 0.343 over 40 training epochs, and average reward on nanoGPT goes 0.194 to 0.246 over 68 epochs (average validation loss 5.150 to 4.066) - while the max reward per epoch fluctuates with no clear upward trend.
  • Mode collapse is diagnosed, not just noted: two common ideas (replace RMSNorm with LayerNorm; EMA over intermediate checkpoints) account for 51 of 128 sampled ideas at epoch 0 and 119 of 128 at epoch 68; thinking traces shrink over training, and ideas with the longest thinking traces consistently have the lowest execution rate.

Key advantages

  • First realistic-scale demonstration that fully open-ended ideas - no constraint on ideation scope, from hyper-parameter tuning to new architectures and objectives - can be implemented, run on real GPUs and scored automatically, with execution rate measured per model rather than assumed.
  • The search scaffold is genuinely sample-efficient: ten epochs of 50 (GRPO) or 80 (nanoGPT) ideas per epoch beat both provided baselines, match or beat the best human expert on the GRPO environment, and beat best-of-N at equal sampling budget - so prior execution trajectories, not sample count, are doing the work.
  • The negative RL result is reported at full strength and mechanistically explained: separating average reward (rises) from max reward (does not) is exactly the distinction that matters for scientific discovery, and the paper grounds it in thinking-length dynamics and per-epoch idea-frequency counts rather than attributing it to noise.
  • Evaluation is defended against reward hacking in a way worth copying: evaluation hyper-parameters are frozen and a token-by-token inference function blocks attention modifications that leak future tokens, an exploit the authors say occurred repeatedly during development and would otherwise have manufactured fake speedup.

Limitations

  • The paper's own first limitation: generalizability is untested. Both environments are single small scales (a 124M and a 1.5B model, one dataset each), and the best-scoring ideas at that scale may not transfer to larger models or other data; the authors note this could be folded into the optimization objective itself.
  • The reward signal is execution-biased. Failed patch generation earns reward 0, so RL is pushed toward simple, reliably-implementable ideas; the authors attribute the diversity collapse to exactly this, noting the executor's capability bounds the experiment scope and injects noise into the reward.
  • RL's ceiling problem is unsolved here: standard GRPO improves average but not upper-bound reward, and all three documented attempts to fix it - appending previous epochs' trajectories to the prompt, a weighted length reward, a weighted similarity penalty - showed no clear gain in early epochs and were early-stopped.
  • Training rewards only effectiveness. Novelty and interestingness are named as complementary objectives the paper does not attempt to measure or optimize, and the executor itself cannot implement ideas needing new dependencies or external tooling.
